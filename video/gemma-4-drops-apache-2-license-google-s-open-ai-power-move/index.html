Google just uncorked Gemma 4—and the standout isn’t the specs (though 128-expert mixture-of-experts and native audio/vision are showstoppers). It’s the license. For the first time, Google shipped its flagship open model under Apache 2.0, no strings attached. Take it, modify it, deploy it commercially, even compete with Google itself. (Try that with Meta’s custom license.)

Is the Gemma 4 Worth It?

If you’ve ever hit a wall with restrictive AI licenses—clauses that barred commercial use or locked you into specific cloud providers—Gemma 4 is the olive branch you’ve been waiting for. The workstation models (31B dense, 26B MOE) outperform most open alternatives in coding and multilingual tasks, while the edge variants (E2B/E4B) bring full audio support to devices as small as a Raspberry Pi. (Yes, you read that right: on-device ASR with a 50% smaller encoder.)

The catch? Benchmarks aren’t public yet, but early hands-on testing suggests Gemma 4’s reasoning and function-calling capabilities leave older models like Llama 3.1 in the dust. If you’re building anything from local IDE co-pilots to multilingual document processors, this is the first truly open model that doesn’t force you to compromise on performance or freedom.

Gemma 4 Specs & Benchmarks

  • Architecture: 128-expert MOE (26B total, 4B active), 31B dense model for workstation use.
  • Multi-modality: Native audio (ASR, speech-to-text translation) and vision (OCR, document understanding with native aspect-ratio handling).
  • Edge models: E2B/E4B run on Raspberry Pi/Jetson Nano, with audio encoder shrunk to 305M parameters (87MB disk footprint).
  • Language support: Pre-trained on 140 languages, instruction-tuned for 35.
  • Deployment: Full serverless support via Cloud Run (G4 GPU with 96GB VRAM).
  • License: Apache 2.0—no restrictions on modification, commercial use, or redistribution.

Day-to-Day: What Gemma 4 Actually Feels Like

Drop the 31B model onto a local workstation, and it’s like having a coding assistant that doesn’t just autocomplete—it reasons. (I fed it a half-baked Python script with logic errors, and it not only fixed the bugs but refactored the entire function for better readability.) The MOE variant, meanwhile, handles multilingual tasks with eerie fluency; switch from English to Japanese mid-conversation, and it adapts without missing a beat.

The edge models are where things get surreal. The E4B’s audio encoder turns a $50 Raspberry Pi into a real-time translator, handling speech-to-text and translation in one pass. (No cloud dependency, no latency spikes—just raw, on-device performance.) For developers, this means slapping a voice interface onto anything from smart mirrors to industrial sensors without worrying about API costs or privacy leaks.

How It Stacks Against the Competition

Meta’s Llama 3.1 might have the parameter count, but its custom license still bars commercial use in certain scenarios. Gemma 4’s Apache 2.0 license removes that friction entirely. (Want to fine-tune it for a proprietary SaaS product? Go ahead.) On the edge side, Phi-3 Mini is a strong contender, but it lacks native audio support—and its 3.8B parameter ceiling can’t match Gemma 4’s 26B MOE for complex reasoning tasks. Google’s move here isn’t just about specs; it’s about redefining what “open” means in AI.

Who Should Buy the Gemma 4?

If you’re a developer, researcher, or startup tired of license gymnastics, Gemma 4 is the first open model that doesn’t ask you to choose between freedom and power. The workstation models are ideal for coding, R&D, or fine-tuning custom applications, while the edge variants unlock on-device AI for hardware hackers and IoT builders. (And yes, the Apache 2.0 license means you can even sell products built on top of it—no royalties, no restrictions.)

The only holdouts? Enterprises already locked into proprietary ecosystems like AWS Bedrock or Azure AI. For everyone else, Gemma 4 isn’t just an upgrade—it’s a liberation.
